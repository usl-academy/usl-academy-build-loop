# Stage 5: Brand Strategy Core Reference

Read this file for the full foundation sprint. It contains the live-research protocol, shared framework definitions, and decision criteria. Naming, deliverables, and the worked example are routed separately from `SKILL.md`.

## 0. Live Research Protocol

Positioning is defined against real alternatives, and market insight concerns what is changing now. Wherever a step is marked **[Live research available]**, use this protocol.

1. **Offer, do not impose.** Ask before researching and proceed on a yes. If the user declines, continue and label affected claims `Unverified: based on user input or model knowledge, not a live check.` If the user says to always research, stop asking for the rest of the session.
2. **Use available web capability.** Use built-in search or an installed search tool. Do not depend on one provider. If none is available, say so once, proceed, and preserve the `Unverified` label.
3. **Search narrowly.** Build 2 to 4 queries from the known audience, category, geography, and question. Batch related needs into one pass.
4. **Prefer primary evidence.** For competitor positioning, use the competitor's own current site or materials. For regulation, advertising policy, trademark sources, and accessibility requirements, use the responsible authority. Use secondary analysis to supplement, not replace, primary evidence.
5. **Synthesize and confirm.** Return a short cited list, distinguish verified facts from inference, and ask the user to confirm or correct before findings govern a decision. Flag stale sources where currency matters.
6. **Record provenance.** Add confirmed findings to `Market Evidence and Sources` with the source, URL, date checked, whether the line is confirmed or inferred, and the decision it informed.
7. **Stay honest.** Research raises confidence; it does not replace the user's market knowledge, create trademark rights, or turn a hypothesis into proof.

### Research points

**Step 3, positioning.** This is the highest-value check. Discover the alternatives buyers actually consider and capture their stated positioning, proof, pricing or format where relevant, and audience. Use this evidence to choose the competitive set, axes, placements, and Point of Difference.

**Step 4, Market Insight.** Check category shifts, regulation, technology, customer behavior, or recent competitor moves. A trend is useful only when it changes the opportunity or required response.

**Step 7, identity direction, optional.** Check competitor color families, type styles, imagery, and common motifs when accidental imitation is a risk. Visual similarity is not the same as legal similarity; this is white-space exploration only.

**Step 8, offer naming.** Check current advertising policy for the category and intended platform. Search shortlisted names and close variants in the relevant category. Then use the full preliminary screen in `references/naming.md`.

**Brand Naming Sprint.** Search shortlisted names, close variants, official trademark sources for the relevant jurisdiction, and only the domains or handles material to launch. Follow `references/naming.md`; never call this legal clearance.

If research changes a placement, insight, or name, show what changed and why.

## 1. Brand Discovery

Brand Discovery uncovers the founding belief, tension, and conviction that give the brand a reason to exist beyond making money. A durable brand has a point of view that can guide difficult decisions, not merely an origin story.

A useful founding conviction is:

- Specific enough to exclude choices
- Relevant to the audience's lived tension
- Durable beyond the current offer
- Believable from the founder or organization's history and behavior

## 2. Brand architecture

Brand architecture defines how this brand relates to parent brands, offers, and sub-brands. Choose it before naming or identity work because the relationship changes how much meaning a name and identity must carry.

- **Standalone brand:** independent name and identity with no necessary parent signal.
- **Masterbrand:** the primary brand under which multiple offers or products sit.
- **Endorsed brand:** a distinct name supported visibly by a parent brand.
- **Sub-brand:** a named expression whose identity and meaning remain closely tied to a parent.
- **Personal brand:** a public identity centered on an individual; offers may use descriptive or distinct names beneath it.

Record what future offers, audiences, or categories the architecture must accommodate. Do not create a sub-brand merely to solve a weak naming decision.

## 3. Iceberg Model and audience architecture

The Iceberg Model maps the user at four layers. Visible demographics and behavior describe context; psychographics, motivations, and emotional needs explain decisions.

- **Demographics:** measurable characteristics such as age, income, location, life stage, or professional role.
- **Behavior:** how the user discovers, buys, uses, delays, substitutes, or decides in the category.
- **Psychographics:** attitudes, values, identity, lifestyle, and worldview.
- **Motivations and needs:** what the user is trying to achieve, protect, become, or avoid.

Build the Iceberg Model for the person experiencing the value. When another person pays or approves, add a Payer Decision Profile instead of blending two decisions into one fictional customer. Capture the payer's trigger, accountable outcome, required proof, objections, authority, approval path, and relationship to the user.

Choose one downstream audience architecture:

- **User-led with payer reassurance:** user experience leads; payer proof supports.
- **Payer-led with user legitimacy:** accountable or economic outcome leads; user adoption proves deliverability.
- **Balanced dual audience:** both participate in one decision and can follow a coherent path with clearly labeled sections.
- **Separate expressions:** user and payer need different entry pages, sales materials, or journeys under one brand.

## 4. Positioning and perceptual maps

Positioning is the deliberate choice of what the brand should mean to a particular audience relative to the alternatives they would otherwise choose.

### Competitive set

Include at least three useful alternatives across:

- Direct competitors solving the problem similarly
- Indirect alternatives solving it another way
- Doing nothing, internal workarounds, or the status quo when these are real choices

Do not populate the map with famous brands the buyer would never consider.

### Axis quality

A defensible pair of axes must be:

- **Buyer-relevant:** each dimension affects preference, trust, adoption, or willingness to pay.
- **Meaningfully independent:** one axis is not a disguised restatement of the other.
- **Observable:** placements can be supported by product behavior, messaging, price, proof, or customer perception.
- **Non-flattering:** the axes are not reverse-engineered simply to isolate the focal brand in the desirable corner.
- **Actionable:** choosing a position changes what the brand says, proves, builds, or refuses.

Use a `-2` to `+2` scale for each axis. Record evidence and confidence for every placement. A map based on the brand's own communications is a **stated-positioning map**; a true **perceptual map** requires audience perception data. Name which one was built.

### Point of Difference and Reasons to Believe

A Point of Difference is a distinctive, relevant idea the brand can credibly support and competitors do not already dominate. It need not be something no competitor could ever claim. Until competitive evidence is checked, label it a positioning hypothesis.

Reasons to Believe make the positioning credible through observable features, process, credentials, proof, heritage, or behavior. Generic quality claims are not reasons to believe.

## 5. Brand House

A Brand House connects the strategic foundation so decisions are not made in isolation.

- **Brand Purpose:** why the brand exists beyond making money; a direction, not a destination.
- **Audience Insight:** a non-obvious truth about the user, and payer where relevant, that changes how the brand should act.
- **Market Insight:** a current shift that creates opportunity or urgency. Label it unverified when it was not checked live.
- **Point of Difference:** the distinctive, supportable territory chosen in positioning.
- **Brand Promise:** the internal commitment every product, service, and communication must honor. It is not a tagline.
- **Brand Values:** principles the brand will preserve under commercial pressure. Phrase them so a real tradeoff is visible.
- **Brand Personality:** human traits that govern recognizable behavior, not merely attractive adjectives.

Test the promise in the user's experience and, when separate, in the payer's decision. A promise that only works as marketing copy is not operational enough.

## 6. Brand Ladder and Brand Idea

The Brand Ladder connects what the product does to the feeling the brand creates:

1. **Product Attributes:** tangible features, methods, or service characteristics.
2. **Functional Benefits:** what those attributes enable the user to do or avoid.
3. **User Benefits:** what the user gains personally or professionally.
4. **Emotional End Benefit:** how the user ultimately feels about themselves or their situation.

Going up, every level should answer `so that`. Going down, every level should answer `because`. Repair any break before choosing the Brand Idea.

The **Brand Idea** is a 2-to-4-word expression of the emotional meaning the brand should create. It is not a tagline, benefit statement, or description of the offer. Test whether it can guide positioning, voice, behavior, and identity without becoming generic inspiration.

## 7. Manifesto

A manifesto declares the worldview the brand and its audience share. It is not an advertisement, mission statement, or feature list.

A strong manifesto:

- Expresses the founding conviction rather than generic optimism
- Makes the right reader feel recognized
- Sounds like the chosen personality and voice
- Ladders to the Brand Idea
- Contains no promise the organization cannot operationally honor

## 8. Identity direction

An identity system is the coherent set of verbal, visual, and behavioral signals that makes the brand recognizable. Stage 5 produces a strategic brief, not finished assets.

### Constraints before aesthetics

Gather:

- Primary touchpoints, environments, and smallest reproduction sizes
- Existing recognition or equity worth preserving
- Production, budget, platform, or packaging constraints
- Accessibility needs, including contrast, legibility, motion, and non-color signaling where relevant
- Operating languages, scripts, localization, and font coverage
- Mandatory, prohibited, or regulated elements

### Coherent routes

Propose two or three routes as complete systems, not disconnected mood-board adjectives. Each route should explain:

- The strategic idea and connection to the Brand Idea
- Logo structure and concept
- Color behavior, including accessibility implications
- Typography roles, legibility, and language support
- Photography or illustration principles and exclusions
- Tone of voice and reader relationship
- Behavior at ordinary and failure moments
- How the route differs from category conventions
- The practical risk or tradeoff

Recommend one route and record why the others were not selected. Avoid universal claims such as `serif means premium`; meaning depends on execution, culture, category, and context.

### Brand behavior

Brand behavior governs actions, especially when the brand is under pressure. Useful principles change service recovery, product decisions, policies, or communication. `Friendly` is a trait; `explain the constraint and offer the next controllable action` is behavior.

## 9. Touchpoints and coherence

A touchpoint is any moment someone encounters the brand: product, website, packaging, sales material, support, invoice, error message, or service interaction. The identity direction should name the first touchpoints where the system must prove itself. Coherence means repeated logic, not identical execution everywhere.
