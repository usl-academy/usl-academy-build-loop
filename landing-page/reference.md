## SECTION 0: LIVE RESEARCH PROTOCOL

Research has three legitimate jobs here: retrieve language or evidence the user does not already possess, reveal category conventions and sameness, and verify current implementation choices. It does not choose a visual identity for the user.

### Protocol

1. **Offer one batch.** Ask once during DIRECT: “I can run one focused pass for missing customer language, category conventions, proof norms, and current implementation options. Want me to?” If the user says to research automatically, do not ask again in the session. The reference board (Section 2.6) is a separate, required step; declining this pass does not skip it.
2. **Use the available web capability.** Do not depend on one provider. If no web access exists, proceed and mark affected findings unverified.
3. **Search narrow.** Use the named buyer, problem, category, geography, traffic source, and intended builder. Four to eight focused queries in one pass are better than four interruptions spread through the page.
4. **Prefer primary evidence.** Buyer communities, review text, official competitor pages, official product documentation, primary research, and current platform documentation outrank affiliate roundups and generic “best landing pages” articles.
5. **Separate observation from prescription.** Report what the category repeats, what visitors may rely on for familiarity, what appears unclaimed, and what the page should refuse. A repeated pattern is not automatically a best practice.
6. **Confirm before use.** The user confirms or corrects findings before they influence copy or design.
7. **Record provenance.** Every external claim used publicly gets its URL and check date in the Sources appendix. Every library or plugin that the build depends on gets an official source in the build sequence.
8. **Stay honest.** Label inference, sample limitations, commercial-source bias, and anything older than roughly twelve months where currency matters.

### Research lanes

| Lane | Example query shape | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Customer language | `[specific problem] forum`, `[category] reviews`, `[persona] complaints` | Exact phrases with source and context; never synthetic quotations |
| Category conventions | `[category] pricing`, `[competitor] landing page`, `[category] demo` | Familiar decision cues, repeated layouts, proof norms, and sameness to avoid |
| Proof | `[problem] primary research`, `[category] industry report`, official case studies | Citable facts or the honest finding that no suitable public proof exists |
| Implementation | Official documentation for the selected builder, primitive library, animation tool, or test tool | Current compatibility, accessibility features, maintenance cost, and source URL |
| Reference field | `[category] [geography] website`, `[category] best designed site`, award galleries filtered to the category, the competitor landscape's named alternatives | The list of pages to capture for the reference board (Section 2.6); never a verdict on what the page should look like |

Do not search “high-converting headline patterns” and copy the resulting formulas. Search the buyer’s language and the category’s visual habits, then make a decision.

## SECTION 1: PAGE STRATEGY AND ARCHITECTURE ROUTER

### 1.1 The six routing inputs

#### Goal

- **Direct purchase:** a visitor can decide and pay on the page.
- **Application or booked call:** the page qualifies and earns a conversation.
- **Lead capture:** the page exchanges a useful asset or next step for contact information.
- **Waitlist:** the page earns belief before the complete product or offer exists.
- **Product adoption:** the visitor should start, install, create, or try.

#### Visitor state

- **Unaware:** does not name the problem yet. Start with a recognizable situation or tension, not product terminology.
- **Problem-aware:** knows the pain, not the available approaches. Establish mechanism and credibility before product detail.
- **Solution-aware:** knows the category. Differentiate the approach and show it.
- **Product-aware:** knows this offer. Reduce risk, answer objections, and make action easy.

#### Trust burden

| Level | Typical conditions | Page consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Low | Free, reversible, familiar, low effort | Short path, product or outcome visible immediately |
| Medium | Meaningful price or time, known category | Mechanism, relevant proof, clear process, objection handling |
| High | Expensive, irreversible, regulated, intimate, novel, or career-affecting | Proof early, authority and boundaries, process transparency, qualification, risk reversal |

#### Demonstration need

- **Low:** the offer is understood through outcome, proof, and process.
- **Medium:** the visitor benefits from a diagram, sample, or before-and-after artifact.
- **High:** the interface, workflow, transformation, or deliverable must be seen to be believed.

#### Audience path

- **Same-person user/payer:** one person experiences value and authorises payment. Do not invent a second role.
- **User-led with payer reassurance:** the user drives discovery; give them a credible value story plus a concise section, artifact, or handoff they can use with the payer.
- **Payer-led with user legitimacy:** the payer drives evaluation; lead with the accountable outcome and proof, then show why users will adopt, trust, and benefit from it.
- **Balanced dual audience:** both roles arrive through the same channel and participate in one decision. Label role-specific modules and avoid competing primary CTAs.
- **Separate expressions:** triggers, vocabulary, proof, or actions differ enough to require separate pages or routes under one brand. Specify each page's visitor, job, and CTA.

The payer may be an employer, parent, procurement lead, insurer, sponsor, manager, partner, or another budget owner. "Customer" is not precise enough when roles differ.

#### Demand orientation and cap status

- **Push:** lead with the specific condition, cost, risk, delay, workaround, or obligation the relevant decision-maker wants to escape. Use observed consequence without exaggerated agitation.
- **Pull:** lead with the experience, identity, access, belonging, mastery, taste, delight, or continuation people voluntarily move toward. Recognition and invitation often outperform a manufactured pain narrative.
- **Active or unknown cap:** constrain public wording to what the evidence supports. Preserve the affected confidence and resolution condition.
- **Paid-traffic readiness:** inherit Stage 4's `ready`, `test-only`, or `not ready` decision. An owned page may still be useful while paid distribution remains unauthorised.

### 1.2 Archetypes

These are starting hypotheses. Modify them when the visitor’s decision requires it.

#### Product or demo-led

Use when the product is visual, the visitor is solution-aware, or demonstration need is high.

Typical sequence:

1. Decision zone with the product in use
2. Demonstration or interactive sample
3. Problem-to-mechanism bridge
4. Use cases or workflow
5. Proof close to the demonstrated claim
6. Pricing or start action
7. Focused objections

Do not place a fake dashboard in a browser frame. Show a real screen, truthful prototype, annotated flow, or honest “concept preview” label.

#### Proof-led

Use when the trust burden is high and credible outcomes exist.

Typical sequence:

1. Decision zone with a restrained claim
2. Strongest relevant proof
3. Buyer situation and cost
4. Mechanism or approach
5. Process and boundaries
6. Additional proof or comparison
7. Offer, qualification, and action

Proof must be specific to the buyer and claim. A logo strip is not proof by itself.

#### Service or application-led

Use for consulting, cohorts, agencies, clinical or professional services, and other offers where fit matters.

Typical sequence:

1. Decision zone with qualification signal
2. Recognizable situation
3. What changes and how the engagement works
4. Who it is and is not for
5. Evidence and authority
6. Process, scope, timing, and price framing
7. Application or call action

Do not disguise a sales call as a diagnostic if it is a sales call.

#### Waitlist

Use when the product or offer is not yet available.

Typical sequence:

1. Tension or future state
2. What is being explored, stated honestly
3. Why this team or founder is pursuing it
4. Evidence that the problem exists
5. What early members receive and what they do not
6. Waitlist action and consent detail

Do not use fake screenshots, invented availability, or implied customer traction.

#### Editorial or founder-led

Use when conviction, taste, expertise, or a category critique is a meaningful part of the reason to believe.

Typical sequence:

1. Strong point of view or observed tension
2. Evidence or story that earned the conviction
3. The category’s default and why it fails
4. The alternative mechanism
5. Offer or product in context
6. Proof, invitation, and action

This is not permission for a long founder autobiography. The visitor remains the subject of the decision.

#### Long-form direct response

Use when the visitor is problem-aware, the decision requires substantial education, the offer is complex, and evidence supports the claims.

Build a narrative, not a repeated stack of headers, cards, and CTAs. Vary section length and form. Place proof at the moment each doubt appears rather than grouping it all under “Testimonials.”

### 1.3 Module selection test

For every proposed module, complete:

> The visitor is asking **[question]**. This module answers it with **[evidence, explanation, or action]**. If omitted, **[specific decision gap]** remains.

If the sentence cannot be completed, omit the module.

### 1.4 Architecture gate

Before writing copy, the architecture must state:

- Selected archetype and why
- Visitor state and traffic source
- Primary action
- Module order
- Visitor question answered by each module
- Modules deliberately omitted
- Where proof appears
- What the visitor sees before the first scroll
- User, payer, budget owner, and approval dependency, with explicit confirmation when they are the same person
- Selected audience path and why one page or multiple pages are appropriate
- Which modules answer the user's decision and which answer the payer's decision
- Desirability orientation and its consequence for the opening motive
- Cap status, claim limits, and paid-traffic readiness

## SECTION 2: EVIDENCE, ASSETS, VOICE, AND REFERENCES

### 2.1 Evidence hierarchy

Use the strongest available source and label it accurately.

1. Buyer behavior, purchases, prior attempts, and owned results
2. Verbatim buyer language with recruitment context
3. Real product or delivery artifacts
4. Primary research and citable category data
5. Founder or team expertise directly relevant to the claim
6. Reasoned hypothesis, clearly labeled

Enthusiasm, likes, impressions, generic testimonials, and visual polish do not promote a claim up this hierarchy.

### 2.2 Asset inventory

| Asset | Exists? | Ownership or permission | What it proves | Page use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Product screens or prototype | | | | |
| Customer artifact or deliverable | | | | |
| Before-and-after evidence | | | | |
| Verbatim quote | | | | |
| Quantitative result | | | | |
| Founder or team photograph | | | | |
| Documentary or process photograph | | | | |
| Diagram, data, or framework | | | | |
| Credential, certification, or press | | | | |

Default asset order:

1. Real product, customer, process, or outcome artifact
2. Purpose-built diagram or data visualization
3. Documentary photography
4. Commissioned or tightly directed illustration
5. Licensed stock with a specific reason
6. Decorative generated imagery

The last item should be rare. Generated imagery cannot substitute for missing evidence.

### 2.3 Claim ledger

Complete before the final copy audit.

| Claim | Decision-maker | Type | Source | Confidence | Cap or limitation | Public wording allowed | Proof placed where |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | user / payer / both | observed / researched / reasoned | | high / medium / low | | | |

No public statistic, comparative price, customer total, result, or scarcity claim can remain source-free.

### 2.4 Voice calibration

Three adjectives are orientation, not a voice. Build a usable brief:

```markdown
**Brand Idea:**
**Reader relationship:** peer / guide / expert / challenger / host / other
**Primary reader:** [user / payer / same person]
**Payer register when separate:** [accountable outcome, proof vocabulary, risk language, and phrases to avoid]
**Three sentences this brand could say:**
1.
2.
3.
**Two sentences it would never say:**
1.
2.
**Vocabulary to use:**
**Vocabulary to avoid:**
**Cadence:**
**Humor:**
**Conviction:**
**Restraint:**
**Punctuation and casing:**
```

The three “could say” sentences should come from existing brand or founder material when possible. If generated during the session, mark them as proposed and confirm them.

### 2.5 Reference deconstruction

References are evidence of decisions, not a shopping catalog.

For each reference, capture:

| Reference | What specifically works | Underlying decision | Transferable principle | What must not be copied |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | |

Also capture one anti-reference:

> We reject **[pattern]** because it makes this brand feel **[wrong quality]** or obscures **[decision]**.

Do not say “make it like Apple,” “make it Stripe-like,” or “use Linear style.” Name the transferable decision: unusually high type contrast, a product demonstration before explanation, documentary photography, dense proof, or restrained motion.

Fill this table from the reference board (2.6), and put the figure number of the capture each row refers to in the first column. A row without a capture is a memory, and memories do not go on the board.

### 2.6 Reference board

The board is the field, looked at. It exists because the quiet failure of this stage is a thesis that cites four good names with one phrase each, gets built faithfully, and still looks like every other page in the category. Nobody captured the pages, so nobody saw that the thesis was the category default with better vocabulary. Written at Step 4, before the thesis, and confirmed by the user before the thesis exists.

The board records decisions, never looks. No capture is copied into the page. The benchmark's palette does not override Stage 5. Forbidden claims from Stage 4 filter every transfer.

#### The field

Twenty to fifty pages. Fewer than twenty and the category default is an impression; more than fifty and nobody reads the board. Draw from, in this order:

1. The buyer's category in the buyer's geography. These are the pages the buyer has already seen, and the page is measured against them whether or not you look.
2. The best of the category worldwide. Where the category's quality ceiling is.
3. The adjacent categories the buyer also sees: the suppliers, the regulators, the tools, the publications.
4. Every alternative named in `usl-build/competitor-landscape.md`.
5. The two pages the user named at Step 1, and any benchmark the user names later. A named benchmark goes to the top of the board, and its measurements lead the findings.

Pages outside the buyer's world (banks, law firms, software companies admired by designers) are excluded unless the user asks for them. They produce a thesis the buyer does not recognise.

#### Capture

- First screen of every page at desktop width (1440 px is the default), dated. Full page for everything shortlisted.
- Save the captures to `usl-build/reference-board/` named by site, and number the figures in the order the board discusses them.
- Record every page that did not render and why: cookie wall, region gate, bot check, blank first paint, access denied. A cookie wall over the hero is a finding about that site, not a reason to skip it.
- Never describe a page from memory or from its own marketing copy. If it cannot be captured, it is listed under "did not render" and nothing is said about its design.

#### Measure the benchmark

For the user's named benchmark, and for each shortlisted page where it matters, read the live stylesheet rather than estimating:

| Measure | Record |
|---|---|
| Type families | How many, which, and which roles each plays |
| Type scale | Headline, section title and body sizes in px |
| Accent colours | How many, and the exact places each is spent |
| Ground and text | The two colours that carry everything else |
| Primary action | The label, its word count, every place it repeats, and what it names: the outcome, the person, the meeting, the channel |
| Shape | Button radius, rule weights, card use, and whether photographs carry text over them |

The measurements are what transfer. "One family at three sizes, body 18 px, one accent spent twice on a whole page, one four-word label in the top bar and the close" is a thesis input. "Calm and premium" is not.

#### State the category default as a count

Look at the first screens together and count what most of them do. "Thirty-one of forty-one open with a full-bleed photograph, a tagline and a coloured button." That count is the anti-reference, verified, and it is the sentence the thesis refuses first. Without the count, the anti-reference is an opinion, and an opinion gets overruled in the build.

#### Shortlist and deconstruct

Six to ten pages that hold up. For each, the Section 2.5 table: what specifically works, the decision underneath it, the transferable principle, what must not be copied, with the figure number. "What must not be copied" is checked against Stage 4's forbidden claims and Stage 5's colour and typography direction before the row is written, so the board never recommends a forbidden thing.

#### Tabulate the action labels

One table across the field: site, primary action label, word count, where it appears, what it names. Put the current or proposed label for this page in the same table so the comparison is visible. Labels in the field cluster at two to four words, a verb and the thing you get; a label that names the channel or carries the product's full name stands out in the table before anyone argues about it.

#### Write the moves

Five to seven concrete changes the thesis will take. Each one names the shortlisted figure it comes from and the Stage 5 rule it was checked against. A move is specific enough to be built: "body to 18 px and a 60-character measure", not "more whitespace". Moves that would break a Stage 4 or Stage 5 rule are listed under "do not copy" instead, so the reader can see they were considered.

#### Record, page and gate

Write `usl-build/6-reference-board.md` with the template in Section 7.4. When someone other than the user will look at the board (the founder, the designer, the client), render it as one self-contained HTML page with the captures embedded, in the shape of the Section 7.4 template, and report where it is published. The markdown is the record; the page is how people look at it. A published example of the shape: https://claude.ai/artifact/XdkzJdDKFZJHmQDep3oLP5 (private until shared by its owner).

Then gate. The user confirms the benchmark, the shortlist, the anti-reference count and the moves. Record what they changed, and only then write the thesis.

#### Currency

A board is evidence of a field at a date. Reuse it for twelve months. After that, or when the category visibly shifts, rebuild it. Refreshing the benchmark row alone is allowed when the user names a new benchmark.

## SECTION 3: VISUAL THESIS AND HERO DIRECTIONS

### 3.1 Visual thesis template

```markdown
**Visual thesis:** [One sentence connecting the brand idea to the visitor’s decision]
**Composition:** [editorial / modular / cinematic / documentary / technical / other, made specific]
**Grid:** [columns, max width, alignment behavior, intentional breaks]
**Typography contrast:** [display versus body relationship, scale, width, casing]
**Density:** [where the page breathes and where it becomes information-dense]
**Color roles:** [background, text, action, proof, state; not merely a palette]
**Image treatment:** [crop, lighting, annotation, frame, texture, captions]
**Signature motif:** [one repeatable device tied to meaning]
**Section transitions:** [how rhythm changes without stacked rectangles]
**Motion rule:** [what moves, why, and what remains static]
**Deliberate imperfection:** [a controlled human or editorial quality]
**Prohibited patterns:** [specific visual defaults this page refuses]
```

Weak thesis: “Modern, clean, premium.”

Useful thesis: “A field notebook for operators: dense proof, annotated real artifacts, narrow editorial type, and one safety-orange action color.”

Every line of the thesis names the board figure it was drawn from or the category default it refuses. A line that names neither is a preference, and preferences are what the build sequence quietly replaces with the template.

### 3.2 The three hero directions

Each direction must differ in composition and first belief, not just wording.

#### Type or editorial-led

Use when the point of view or promise is the sharpest asset. Control line breaks deliberately. Pair the statement with a small, concrete proof or qualification signal so it does not become a poster.

#### Product, artifact, or demonstration-led

Use when seeing is believing. The visual is primary evidence, not decoration. Annotate what the visitor should notice. Do not wrap every screenshot in identical browser chrome.

#### Proof, story, or tension-led

Use when trust or recognition must precede explanation. Start with an observed result, a customer artifact, a concrete situation, or a founder-earned tension. Do not begin with an unsupported dramatic claim.

### 3.3 Genericness test

Remove the logo, product name, and final copy. Then ask:

- Is the composition still tied to this brand’s behavior?
- Does the principal asset come from this product or market?
- Does the page use a motif with meaning, or a decoration with fashion?
- Could the same direction sell payroll software, protein powder, and an AI agent with only a color change?

If the last answer is yes, revise before writing the page.

### 3.4 Rhythm rules

- Alternate information density intentionally. Do not repeat the same padding and card count in every section.
- Let at least one important section escape the standard container when the thesis justifies it.
- Use cards only for genuinely separable objects or choices.
- A divider, change in typographic scale, image crop, caption system, or grid shift can create rhythm without another colored rectangle.
- Repetition should establish identity or comparison, not reveal the component library.
- Mobile is a recomposition, not a narrow desktop stack. Preserve hierarchy, not geometry.

## SECTION 4: MODULAR COPY AND EXPERIENCE GUIDANCE

These modules are ingredients. Select and order them through Section 1.

### 4.1 Decision zone

**Visitor question:** Am I in the right place, what changes for me, and what can I do?

Include the smallest combination that answers:

- Who or what situation this is for
- Specific outcome or useful tension
- The offer or mechanism, when the visitor needs category clarity
- Primary action
- One supporting proof, qualification, or risk-reducer when needed

Avoid:

- Empty transformation verbs
- A headline that a competitor can inherit unchanged
- Multiple equal CTAs
- Badges and announcement pills with no decision value
- Decorative dashboards unrelated to the promise

### 4.2 Context or problem

**Visitor question:** Do you understand what is actually happening?

Use when recognition must precede explanation. Build from a concrete trigger, failed workaround, cost, or sequence. Do not agitate emotions beyond the evidence. “You language” is useful when natural, not mandatory in every sentence.

Possible forms:

- A short observed scene
- An annotated broken workflow
- A “what happens now” sequence
- Customer phrases against the business’s old language
- A cost or consequence table

### 4.3 Mechanism or solution

**Visitor question:** Why would this produce a different result?

Name the causal mechanism, not only the desired after-state. Distinguish it from both the status quo and named alternatives. If the mechanism is still a hypothesis, say so.

Avoid “There is a better way” unless the next sentence makes the mechanism concrete enough to earn it.

### 4.4 Demonstration

**Visitor question:** Can I see how this works or what I receive?

Use real screens, a prototype, sample output, annotated deliverable, workflow, calculator, recording, or before-and-after artifact. Label concepts and simulated data honestly.

The demonstration must explain one meaningful task or transition. A carousel of tiny screenshots is not a demonstration.

### 4.5 Components, features, or deliverables

**Visitor question:** What is included, and why does each part exist?

Connect each component to a named obstacle or outcome. Do not automatically render three or six cards. Choose the form that matches the material:

- Sequential steps
- Annotated single system
- Comparison table
- Editorial list
- Expandable technical detail
- Demonstrated workflow

If a component cannot connect to a buyer obstacle, question whether it belongs on the page.

### 4.6 Proof

**Visitor question:** Why should I believe this claim for someone like me?

Proof belongs next to the claim it supports. Available forms:

- Specific outcome with baseline, interval, and context
- Verbatim quotation with role and recruitment context
- Before-and-after artifact
- Product behavior demonstrated live
- Current spend or prior attempts that validate the problem
- Relevant founder or team proof
- Primary research with citation
- Honest pilot, waitlist, or usage count

Logos, star ratings, and “trusted by” language without context are weak. Never invent names, quotes, counts, or results in a worked example that the runtime might mistake for user evidence.

### 4.7 Process

**Visitor question:** What happens after I act?

Use when delivery, onboarding, application, or implementation creates uncertainty. State steps, owner, timing, required input, and visible result. Avoid a decorative three-step row that omits the work the buyer must do.

### 4.8 Comparison or alternatives

**Visitor question:** Why this instead of what I already do?

Compare against real alternatives, including doing nothing. Use dimensions the buyer actually cares about. Do not create a rigged feature matrix where the product wins every row.

### 4.9 Founder or authority

**Visitor question:** Why are you qualified to make this judgment or deliver this result?

Use only relevant experience, proof, and conviction. Connect biography to the buyer’s risk. A portrait plus a career summary is not automatically credibility.

### 4.10 Offer and pricing

**Visitor question:** What exactly am I committing to, what does it cost, and what reduces my risk?

Include the confirmed offer, price, payment terms, guarantee, scope, timing, and legitimate scarcity. Values marked as estimates upstream must not appear publicly until verified.

Price visibility depends on the sales model, not copywriter confidence. Direct purchase usually requires price. Enterprise or custom work may require a qualified range or an honest explanation of how pricing is set.

### 4.10A Payer decision and approval

**Payer question:** Why should I authorise or fund this for someone else, what result am I accountable for, and what happens next in the approval process?

Use when payer and user differ. It may be a compact reassurance block, an employer or parent handoff artifact, a procurement section, or a separate page. Include only what the decision requires:

- Payer proposition and accountable outcome
- Evidence at the payer's required standard
- User adoption, consent, or experience safeguards
- Price, funding, reimbursement, procurement, or approval mechanics
- Risk, responsibility, privacy, and boundary information
- The payer's honest next action and what follows

Do not make the user perform procurement copy, and do not reduce the user to an ROI unit. If payer and user incentives conflict, name the boundary rather than writing around it.

### 4.11 Qualification

**Visitor question:** Is this for me, and will you say no when it is not?

Use for high-trust services, applications, regulated categories, and constrained cohorts. State both fit and disqualifiers. Qualification is more credible than universal relevance.

### 4.12 FAQ or objection handling

**Visitor question:** What unresolved issue could still stop me?

Use the objections in their observed order. Four genuine questions beat six generic placeholders. Answers should be direct, bounded, and consistent with the offer.

Do not include “What are the benefits?” or “Why choose us?” unless a buyer actually asks it.

### 4.13 Closing action

**Visitor question:** What exactly should I do now, and what happens next?

Restate the action with appropriate motivation or risk reduction. Do not manufacture urgency. The button text must describe the action honestly. “Get started” is acceptable when starting is literally what happens; absolute bans create awkward copy.

## SECTION 5: AUTHORSHIP AND ANTI-TEMPLATE AUDITS

### 5.1 Authored-copy audit

Score each item `0`, `1`, or `2`.

| Test | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Source traceability | Claims or language invented | Mixed or unclear sources | Every meaningful claim traces to evidence or is labeled as hypothesis |
| Competitor replacement | Most copy survives a logo swap | Some distinctive details | Core language and mechanism belong to this buyer, offer, and brand |
| Specificity | Abstract benefits and adjectives | Some concrete detail | Concrete triggers, actions, mechanisms, and consequences dominate |
| Cadence | Repeated fragments or uniform sentences | Mostly natural | Deliberate variation that matches the voice brief |
| Vocabulary | Generic marketing language | Mostly on-brand | Uses confirmed customer and brand vocabulary without parroting |
| Claim-to-proof fit | Proof absent or mismatched | Proof grouped elsewhere | Proof sits next to the claim and matches its scope |
| Objection fidelity | Generic FAQ | Some real objections | Real objections appear in observed order and language |
| Action clarity | Vague or competing actions | Clear but under-explained | One honest action with a clear next state |
| Decision-role coverage | Payer or user is missing, or their motives are blended | Both appear but the path is unclear | User and payer each receive the right proposition, proof, objections, and action; same-person cases are explicit |

Passing threshold: `15/18`, with no zero in Source traceability, Claim-to-proof fit, or Decision-role coverage when payer and user differ. When they are the same person, a `2` requires that this is stated explicitly and the page does not invent an approval path.

### 5.2 AI-copy pattern sweep

Flag and rewrite unless the phrase is genuinely in the brand’s source material:

- “Unlock,” “unleash,” “revolutionize,” “transform your business,” “next level,” “cutting-edge,” “seamless,” “effortless”
- “Not X. Not Y. Just Z.”
- “It is not just X. It is Y.”
- “Whether you are A, B, or C”
- “Imagine a world where...”
- “Designed to empower...”
- Three dramatic sentence fragments in a row
- Repeated one-sentence paragraphs used as artificial intensity
- Rhetorical-question stacks
- Unsupported “finally,” “the only,” “everything you need,” or “built for modern teams”
- Abstract nouns where an action, object, interval, or consequence could be named

Do not mechanically remove all fragments or parallelism. Remove the unearned rhythm.

### 5.3 Visual-authorship audit

Score each `0`, `1`, or `2`.

| Test | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Composition | Stock block stack | Some custom composition | Layout expresses the visual thesis and decision order |
| Asset authenticity | Decorative or fake imagery | Mixed | Principal visuals are real, owned, or purpose-built evidence |
| Typography | Default scale and width | Customized | Type hierarchy and line breaks create a recognizable editorial voice |
| Rhythm | Repeated containers and padding | Some variation | Density, alignment, and transitions vary with meaning |
| Component restraint | Pills, cards, and badges everywhere | Mostly controlled | Components represent real objects and choices |
| Color behavior | Generic gradient or palette application | Brand colors applied | Color roles communicate action, proof, and state |
| Motion | Decorative or excessive | Limited | Motion explains hierarchy or causality and respects user preference |
| Mobile authorship | Desktop stacked narrowly | Mostly adapted | Hierarchy and interaction are intentionally recomposed |

Passing threshold: `13/16`, with no zero in Asset authenticity or Mobile authorship.

### 5.4 Red-flag inventory

Count and justify every occurrence of:

- Centered hero with badge, gradient headline, two buttons, and floating dashboard
- Purple or blue glow with no brand reason
- Bento grid used for unrelated features
- Every object inside a rounded card
- Identical icon circles above every heading
- Logo strip presented as outcome proof
- Fake browser chrome around every screenshot
- Infinite marquee with no informational value
- Animated beams, particles, or orbs unrelated to the mechanism
- Excessive pill labels
- Alternating white and tinted rectangles with identical internal layout

Zero occurrences is not the goal. Intentional use is. Unexplained repetition fails.

## SECTION 6: IMPLEMENTATION-SYSTEM SELECTOR

### 6.1 General selection rules

1. Use the existing project stack when one exists and is serviceable.
2. Choose tools for accessibility, maintenance, and required behavior before visual novelty.
3. Do not install a library for buttons, cards, or headings the project already owns.
4. Use one primitive system, not several overlapping ones.
5. Component examples are structural references. Recompose them against the visual thesis.
6. Verify current versions and official compatibility before naming installation commands.

### 6.2 Custom React or Next.js

Useful categories:

| Need | Appropriate choice | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Accessible behavior | Base UI, Radix Primitives, or the project’s existing accessible primitives | Prefer unstyled behavior; choose one system |
| Owned component distribution | A project-specific shadcn-compatible registry | Use for repeated internal patterns, not a stock visual identity |
| Styling | Existing CSS architecture, CSS Modules, or Tailwind when already used | Encode tokens and layout rules before sections |
| Motion | Native CSS or Motion for React | Use only for hierarchy, state, or causality; support reduced motion |
| Visual regression | Playwright screenshots or the project’s existing visual test system | Capture stable desktop and mobile states |
| Component QA | Storybook when the project maintains a reusable component system | Avoid adding it solely for one static page |
| Automated audit | Lighthouse or the project’s equivalent | Set proportionate performance, accessibility, and SEO gates |

Do not default to highly styled marketing packs. If a distinctive third-party block is used, inspect its code, accessibility, payload, mobile behavior, and visual signature first.

### 6.3 Framer

- Create color, type, spacing, and radius styles before sections.
- Build reusable components only for repeated, meaningfully identical elements.
- Use native breakpoints and accessibility controls.
- Relume or marketplace blocks may accelerate wireframing. Replace their composition and styling before publication.
- Avoid stacking effects that are difficult to maintain or inaccessible outside pointer interaction.
- Preview at real breakpoints and inspect the published URL, not only the canvas.

### 6.4 Webflow

- Establish variables, type styles, container behavior, and spacing conventions first.
- Use components for repeated structures and CMS for genuinely repeatable content such as resources or a large FAQ.
- Relume can accelerate structural exploration. It is not a finished art direction.
- Check desktop, tablet, mobile landscape, and mobile portrait.
- Verify semantic element choices, focus states, form success and error states, and published performance.

### 6.5 Carrd

- Use for short, focused pages whose conversion and maintenance needs fit the platform.
- Prefer one strong composition, real imagery, controlled typography, and a working form over simulated application complexity.
- Avoid fragile custom code unless the user can maintain it.
- Verify the actual mobile publication and form destination.

### 6.6 WordPress

- Use the existing block editor, theme system, or one established page builder. Do not mix builders.
- Create global styles and reusable patterns before page assembly.
- Limit plugin additions and record why each is required.
- Test caching, forms, consent, responsive images, keyboard navigation, and plugin conflicts on the rendered site.

### 6.7 Reference libraries

- **Relume:** wireframes and structural exploration across Figma, Webflow, and React.
- **Mobbin or equivalent real-interface libraries:** study real composition and flows, not component code.
- **Official builder galleries:** inspect platform-native behavior and maintenance constraints.

Reference libraries answer “what decisions exist?” They do not answer “what should this brand look like?” They do not replace the reference board either: the board is the buyer's actual field, captured and dated, and a library is everyone's.

## SECTION 8: VERIFICATION STANDARD

### 8.1 Functional

- Primary CTA goes to the intended destination.
- When payer and user differ, each intended route and CTA reaches the correct next state; approval handoffs preserve context and do not expose private user information.
- Forms validate required fields, expose errors accessibly, prevent duplicate submissions where necessary, and show a clear success state.
- Analytics records the primary action and meaningful funnel events without collecting more personal data than intended.
- Navigation, accordions, media controls, pricing toggles, and external links work.

### 8.2 Content and claims

- Published copy matches the approved document unless a change is logged.
- Every public claim is supported at its actual scope.
- Concepts, prototypes, estimates, and illustrative data are labeled.
- Price, guarantee, timing, availability, and qualification match the current offer.
- User and payer propositions, proof, objections, and approval mechanics match the confirmed audience architecture.
- Public claims stay within inherited caps and paid-traffic readiness; no cap is silently treated as released.
- No placeholder proof, dates, counts, or URLs remain.

### 8.3 Accessibility

- One logical `h1`, coherent heading order, landmarks, labels, and semantic controls
- Complete keyboard path with visible focus
- Sufficient contrast for text, controls, and state
- Alternative text based on image purpose; decorative images ignored correctly
- Error messages associated with fields
- Motion respects reduced-motion preferences
- Touch targets and text remain usable on small screens
- No meaning depends only on color, hover, or animation

### 8.4 Performance

- Responsive, correctly sized images in modern formats where supported
- No unnecessary video, animation, font weight, or component-library payload
- Stable initial layout with reserved media dimensions
- Critical content and CTA do not wait on decorative JavaScript
- Performance is measured on the rendered page, not inferred from source

Set numerical budgets only when the platform and project make them meaningful. Verify current measurement guidance before hardcoding targets.

### 8.5 Responsive and visual

- Inspect at desktop, tablet, and at least one narrow mobile viewport
- No horizontal overflow or clipped controls
- Mobile hierarchy is intentional, not merely stacked
- Line lengths, headline wraps, and image crops remain deliberate
- Screenshots capture the entire page and the decision zone separately
- Visual regression baselines are recorded when the project uses them
- The page scores at least `13/16` on both authorship audits

### 8.6 SEO and sharing

- Descriptive title and meta description
- Canonical URL where appropriate
- Social sharing title, description, and image
- Indexing behavior matches the launch stage
- Structured data only when accurate and supported
- Search optimization beyond the page build is separate post-launch work, not part of this stage

### 8.7 Final human questions

1. What on this page could belong only to this business?
2. What is the strongest piece of evidence, and is it visible soon enough?
3. Where does the page repeat itself instead of advancing the decision?
4. Which visual choice exists because a library made it easy rather than because the thesis required it?
5. If motion, gradients, and rounded cards disappeared, would the page still have an identity?
6. If payer and user differ, can each person tell what decision is theirs, what evidence applies to them, and what happens after they act?
7. Placed beside the board's category-default captures, does the rendered first screen read as one of them?

## SECTION 9: ARCHITECTURE EXAMPLES

These examples calibrate routing, not copy. Do not reuse their content.

### Example A: B2B workflow product

**Situation:** Solution-aware operations lead arriving from a comparison article. Medium trust burden. High demonstration need. Trial CTA.

**Route:** Product or demo-led.

**Modules:** Product-in-use decision zone → annotated workflow → current workaround comparison → role-specific use cases → proof beside time-saving claim → start trial → technical FAQ.

**Visual thesis:** An operations console, not an AI spectacle. Dense real screens, precise annotations, one alert color, minimal motion showing the workflow transition.

**Omitted:** Long emotional problem section, founder story, generic feature cards.

### Example B: High-trust advisory service

**Situation:** Founder referred by a peer. Problem-aware. High trust burden. Application CTA.

**Route:** Proof-led plus service/application-led.

**Modules:** Restrained outcome and qualification → relevant case artifact → situation and financial consequence → advisory mechanism → process and boundaries → who it is not for → engagement options → application.

**Visual thesis:** An annotated case file. Documentary artifacts, narrow editorial typography, visible assumptions, no stock photography, no animated counters.

**Omitted:** Pricing-card comparison, logo marquee, generic FAQ.

### Example C: Consumer waitlist

**Situation:** Problem-aware community visitor. Product not built. Low monetary risk but low patience. Waitlist CTA.

**Route:** Waitlist with editorial/founder support.

**Modules:** Recognizable moment → honest concept and why now → participant observations → founder relevance → what early members receive → consent-aware waitlist form.

**Visual thesis:** A field journal from the community. Real participant environments with permission, captions as evidence, warm restrained type, no fake app interface.

**Omitted:** Price, guarantee, fabricated testimonial, fake product tour.

### Example D: Local trade service

**Situation:** Product-aware homeowner arriving from local search with an urgent problem. Medium trust burden. Call or quote CTA. Low demonstration need.

**Route:** Short service-led page.

**Modules:** Service, location, and availability decision zone → real project photographs → licenses, scope, and response expectation → short process → review excerpts with context → call or quote action.

**Visual thesis:** Evidence from the worksite. Real materials, dates, locations, and clean practical typography. No abstract icons, glossy gradients, or invented “premium” language.

**Omitted:** Long manifesto, component grid, elaborate animation, eight-section sales narrative.
