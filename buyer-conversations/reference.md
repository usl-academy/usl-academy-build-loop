# Buyer Conversations
## Knowledge File: Rules, Recruiting, the Arc, Capture, Signal, Templates, and Examples

---

## SECTION 0: LIVE RESEARCH PROTOCOL

This skill's raw material is what real people said to the user, which no search can retrieve. Research therefore has one legitimate use here and one only: **substituting for conversations the user cannot get**, in Track B. Wherever a step is marked **[Live research available]**, follow this protocol.

**1. Offer, do not impose.** Ask before researching, then proceed on a yes. If the user declines, proceed on what exists and mark anything affected "Unverified: based on model knowledge, not a live check." If they say to always research, stop asking and run it automatically at each marked point for the rest of the session.

**2. Use whatever web capability is available.** Built-in web search and fetch, or an installed tool if the environment has one (Firecrawl, Exa, Jina). Do not depend on any single tool. If none is available, say so once and proceed. Never block the debrief waiting on a tool.

**3. Search narrow.** Two to four targeted queries built from the persona, problem, category and geography. One round, then synthesize.

**4. Attribute everything.** Every line lifted from a public source carries the source URL and the date checked. In this skill that is not bookkeeping: Stage 4 weights an observed quote differently from a forum post, and it can only do that if the difference is visible in the file.

**5. Never launder a public source into a quote.** A sentence from a review site is `[public source]` with its URL. A sentence a buyer said to the user is a quote. These do not mix, they do not get merged into one list, and a `[public source]` line never appears in the verbatim quotes section without its tag.

### Research scope for this skill

**One marked point: Track B, Section 7.** When the user genuinely cannot run conversations, public sources carry the language table. Query shapes that work: `"[problem]" site:reddit.com`, `[category] reviews "switched from"`, `[tool] alternatives complaints`, `[role] forum "how do you handle"`. Complaint threads, one-star and three-star reviews, and support communities outperform anything written by a vendor.

Do **not** research in Phases 1 to 3 when live conversations are happening. Prepping questions from search results produces a guide about the category rather than about this buyer, and reading the market before the cross-read contaminates the pattern the captures were supposed to reveal on their own.

## SECTION 1: THE PAST-NOT-FUTURE RULE

**Ask about the past, not the future.** Everything in this skill follows from this.

Future questions get predictions. People cannot predict their own behaviour, and they are especially bad at it in front of someone visibly hoping for a particular answer. Past questions get facts. What someone actually did last month is not an opinion.

### The substitution table

Convert every hypothetical the user proposes before it reaches a buyer.

| Do not ask | Ask instead |
|---|---|
| Would you use something that did X? | Walk me through the last time you dealt with this. What did you actually do? |
| Would you pay for this? | What are you spending on this now? Tools, headcount, time, workarounds. |
| Does this sound useful? | What have you already tried? What happened? |
| How big a problem is this? | When did it last happen? What did it cost you? |
| Who would buy this? | Who would have to approve spending money on this? |
| Would you want it to do Y? | When it happens, what do you wish existed? |
| Is this a priority for you? | What did you work on instead, the last time this came up? |
| Would you switch? | What made you switch, the last time you changed how you do this? |

### "That sounds useful" is the most expensive sentence in the week

It feels like validation and it costs nothing to say. People are polite, especially to someone they like, and especially about something that person is visibly excited about.

The test: if the user leaves a conversation encouraged and cannot point to a specific thing that happened to a specific person on a specific date, they learned nothing. Encouragement is not data. Say this to the user in the debrief every single time it comes up, because it does not stop feeling like validation just because they have been warned once.

## SECTION 2: RECRUITING

The hard part is never the conversation. It is the calendar.

**Target ten asks for five conversations, as the default.** Send them all at once rather than one at a time waiting for replies. Sequential asking is how a week produces two conversations.

**The adjustment rule.** For enterprise, regulated, and rare buyers, ten asks may be a quarter's networking rather than a week's messaging, and three conversations with real budget owners beat five with whoever was reachable. Adjust the target openly: say what it now is and why. What does not move is the cross-read arithmetic — three is still an anecdote and five is still a pattern — so a reduced target is a trade the user makes knowingly, and the Section 5 cross-read carries the caveat.

### The ask that works

> "I am researching how [role] handle [problem area]. I am not selling anything, I do not have anything to sell. Would you have twenty minutes to tell me how you deal with it?"

Three things are doing work:

- **"Researching"** sets the frame as learning rather than selling. It is also true.
- **"I do not have anything to sell"** removes the defence. Say it only if it is true.
- **"Twenty minutes"** is small enough to say yes to and long enough to be useful. Do not ask for an hour.

### The ask that does not work

> "I am building something that helps [role] with [problem]. Would love to get your thoughts and show you what we are working on."

That schedules a demo. They arrive prepared to be encouraging, and they succeed.

### The honest variant, when something does exist

If the user already has a product, a waitlist, or a landing page, the line about having nothing to sell is false and using it will cost them the relationship when the buyer finds out. Use this instead:

> "I am working on something in this space, and I have deliberately stopped building to go and talk to people first. I am not going to pitch you or show you anything. I want twenty minutes on how you handle it today."

It keeps the learning frame and it survives contact with the truth.

### Warm routes, in order

1. The Conversation access list in `usl-build/0-expertise-map.md`, if Stage 0 was run: counts, roles, and relationships recorded for exactly this purpose. (A v1 Stage 0 packet has no such section; its Access lens answers serve the same role.)
2. Former colleagues who moved to other companies. Underrated, because they owe the user nothing and no longer share an employer.
3. People who have asked the user about this exact topic before. They self-identified.
4. Communities where the user is a member rather than a marketer.
5. Introductions. **"Who else deals with this that I should talk to?"** at the end of every conversation is how five becomes eight. Ask it every time, including after a weak conversation.

Cold outreach is last, not first, and it converts at a rate that makes ten asks into one conversation rather than five.

## SECTION 3: THE QUESTION ARC

**There are two arcs. Pick the one that matches the Desirability rubric recorded in the Stage 1 packet.** The push arc below is the default and covers most cases. Where Stage 1 recorded the **pull** rubric, use the pull arc further down instead: it asks the same past-not-future questions about a buyer who is reaching for something rather than escaping it. Asking a pull buyer what frustrates them about the category produces a polite shrug, and a week of shrugs reads as a kill signal when it is only a wrong question.

Where the payer is not the person feeling the problem, both arcs keep questions 6 and 7 for the payer and can drop them from the feeler's call. See the payer arc at the end of this section.

---

### THE PUSH ARC (default)

Twenty minutes, nine questions, one arc. The user will not get through all nine every time, and that is fine. **Questions 2, 4 and 6 must happen.**

Each question below has its follow-up for when the answer is vague. The follow-up is not optional. It is where the answer actually lives.

**1. Context.** "Tell me about your role, and where [problem area] sits in it."
*Warm up. Also tells the user whether they are talking to the right person at all.*
Follow-up when vague: "How much of your week touches that?"

**2. The last instance.** "Walk me through the last time you dealt with this. What actually happened?"
*The single most important question. Everything after it builds on the specific story.*
Follow-up when vague: "When was that, roughly? Take me through it from the start." If they cannot produce an instance at all, that is the kill signal, and it is a finding rather than a failed question.

**3. The cost.** "What did that cost you? Time, money, or something else?"
*Turns a story into a number. Push gently for a figure, even a rough one.*
Follow-up when vague: "Ballpark. Was that an afternoon or a fortnight?"

**4. What they have tried.** "What have you already tried? What happened?"
*The best question in the set. Nobody tries things to fix a mild annoyance, so this measures real severity. It also hands over their objections in advance, and names the real competitor, which is usually a spreadsheet rather than the company the user was worried about.*
Follow-up when vague: "Even something that did not work. What did you rig up?"

**5. Current state.** "So what are you doing about it right now?"
*The status quo is the actual competitor. Understand it properly.*
Follow-up when vague: "Walk me through what happens today, step by step."

**6. Spend.** "What are you spending on this at the moment? Tools, headcount, your own time, workarounds?"
*Ability to pay, evidenced rather than assumed. The question that most changes the Week 3 market screen.*
Follow-up when vague: "Is anything on this being paid for out of a budget line, or is it all absorbed?"

**7. Authority.** "If you decided to spend money fixing this, who would have to agree?"
*Reveals the gap between who feels the pain and who controls the budget. That gap is the most common reason a genuinely good problem is a bad business.*
Follow-up when vague: "Has anything like that been signed off before? Who signed it?"

**8. Severity.** "On a scale where one is mildly annoying and five is I need this fixed this quarter, where does this sit for you?"
*A number comparable across conversations. Ask near the end, after the story.*
Follow-up: none. Take the number and note it against the story, which often justifies a higher one than they give.

**9. Referral.** "Who else deals with this that I should talk to?"
*How five conversations become eight.*

### Six rules for the twenty minutes

1. **Do not pitch.** Not at the start, ideally not at all. The moment the user describes what they are building, the buyer starts being helpful, and helpful people stop being accurate. If asked directly: "I would rather hear how you handle it first, then I will tell you what I am thinking", and come back to it at the end.
2. **Do not lead.** "That must be frustrating" tells them the answer. "What was that like?" does not.
3. **Shut up.** After asking, stop. The useful answer usually comes after the uncomfortable pause, and it is almost always the second thing they say.
4. **Never accept an adjective without an instance.** "It is a nightmare" is not data. "What happened?" is the follow-up, every time.
5. **Follow the emotion.** When the tone changes, stop working the list and ask what just happened there. That is where the real problem lives.
6. **Ask "and then what happened?" repeatedly.** Most stories stop three steps before the interesting part.

### THE PULL ARC

For offers people move toward rather than away from: publications, communities, media, courses and education, games, entertainment, consumer products bought for enjoyment, and aspirational or identity-driven products. Nothing is broken, so do not hunt for a break. You are measuring attention, taste, habit, and what they have actually paid for.

The past-not-future rule is unchanged and matters more here, not less. "Would you subscribe to that?" is the single most misleading question available in a pull category, because saying yes is free and pleasant. **Questions 2, 3 and 5 must happen.**

**1. Context.** "How did you get into [category]? How long have you been into it?"
*Establishes whether this is a passing interest or an identity. The difference decides everything downstream.*
Follow-up when vague: "What got you started?"

**2. The last thing they consumed.** "What is the last thing in this space you actually read, watched, played, or went to? Walk me through it."
*The equivalent of the push arc's last instance, and the same load-bearing question. Behaviour, not preference.*
Follow-up when vague: "When was that? What made you pick that one?"

**3. What they have paid for.** "What have you paid for in this space? Subscriptions, memberships, courses, events, merchandise, anything."
*The one that decides the score. Free consumption is not demand in a pull category, and this question is what separates a 2 from a 4.*
Follow-up when vague: "Anything at all, even once, even small? What made that one worth paying for?"

**4. What they abandoned.** "What have you subscribed to or joined and then stopped? Why?"
*The pull equivalent of "what have you tried". Churn reasons are the objections, handed over in advance, and they are the hardest thing to get any other way.*
Follow-up when vague: "What was the moment you stopped opening it?"

**5. Their current diet.** "What do you follow regularly now? Where do you actually spend the attention?"
*The real competitor is not another product, it is whatever currently owns the slot. Name it specifically.*
Follow-up when vague: "What did you open most recently, before this conversation?"

**6. What they tell other people about.** "What in this space have you recommended to someone else, or shared?"
*Advocacy is the strongest pull signal available and it is nearly impossible to fake in retrospect. It also reveals what they think the good stuff is.*
Follow-up when vague: "When did you last send something to someone?"

**7. The gap.** "Is there anything you wish existed here and cannot find?"
*The only question in either arc that points forward, and it is placed late deliberately, after their behaviour is on the record. Treat the answer as a lead, never as evidence.*
Follow-up when vague: "What do you end up settling for instead?"

**8. Identity.** "How would you describe yourself to someone else in this space? Are you a [category] person?"
*Identity attachment is what makes pull spending durable and premium. Listen for the noun they choose for themselves; it is often the best positioning language the week produces.*
Follow-up: none. Take the phrasing verbatim.

**9. Referral.** "Who else is into this that I should talk to?"

**Reading the pull arc.** Strong signal is a specific recent thing consumed, something they have actually paid for, a named thing they abandoned and why, and something they have recommended unprompted. Weak signal is broad enthusiasm for the category with no payment, no churn story, and no recommendation. "I love this stuff" with nothing paid for is the pull equivalent of "that sounds useful", and it is scored the same way.

### THE PAYER ARC

Run this when Stage 1 recorded a payer who is not the person feeling the problem. It is short, and it does not need the problem explained: the payer usually knows less about the problem and more about the money, and treating them as a second feeler wastes the call.

**1. Scope.** "What sits in your budget for [area]?"
**2. The last approval.** "What is the last thing in this area you signed off on? Walk me through how that happened."
*The load-bearing question. It reveals the real process, the real number, and the real trigger.*
Follow-up when vague: "Who brought it to you, and what made it a yes?"
**3. The last rejection.** "What is the last thing like this you turned down, and why?"
*Objections, in advance, from the person who actually raises them.*
**4. What it competes with.** "If this got funded, what would not get funded?"
*Budget is never assessed in isolation and this is the question that surfaces the real comparison.*
**5. The trigger.** "What would have to be happening for this to become urgent rather than sensible?"
*The one permitted near-hypothetical, and only because the answer is about their process rather than their behaviour. Follow it immediately with: "Has that happened before? What did you do?"*
**6. The process.** "If someone wanted this, what would they actually have to do to get it approved? Who else signs?"

**Reading the payer arc.** Strong signal is a specific recent approval with a number, a named process, and a clear trigger. Weak signal is "we would look at anything that saves time", which is what people say when nothing is going to be approved.

## SECTION 4: THE CAPTURE FORMAT

**This is the deliverable.** The conversation is not the output; this is. Filled within ten minutes of hanging up, never at the end of the day.

Every field exists because a specific Week 3 step consumes it. Tell the user this, because it is what makes the tedious parts worth doing:

| Field | What it feeds in Week 3 |
|---|---|
| Verbatim quotes | Stage 4 Step 3, the avatar language table |
| What they tried, and what happened | Stage 4 Step 3, objections and distrust |
| Objections in the order they surfaced | Stage 4 Step 3. Order matters as much as content, because it sets copy sequence |
| Current cost, in time or money | Stage 4 Step 1 market screen, and Step 10 value anchoring |

## SECTION 5: SIGNAL READING AND THE CROSS-READ

### Per conversation

**Strong signal**

- They have **already tried to solve it.** Any attempt, however crude, is the clearest evidence of real pain.
- They are **spending money on it now.** Tools, contractors, headcount, or their own senior time.
- They can **name who approves budget** without hesitating.
- They **remembered a specific instance**, with a date or a rough one.
- They asked **when it would be available.**

**Weak signal**

- "That sounds useful." "We would definitely look at that." "Let me know when it is ready."
- No prior attempts to solve it.
- They cannot remember the last specific instance.
- Enthusiasm about the idea, vagueness about the situation.

**The rule:** three or more weak markers and no strong markers is a weak conversation, regardless of how pleasant it was.

**The kill signal:** they cannot remember the last time it happened. **If the problem does not generate memories, it does not generate budget.** One instance of this is noise. Across most of the set it is the week's answer.

### Across all of them

1. **LANGUAGE.** Two columns: the words they actually used, against the words the user has been using. Mark how many conversations each phrase appeared in. A phrase in three or more is a headline, not a quote.
2. **PATTERN.** What did most of them have in common that the user did not expect?
3. **DIVERGENCE.** Where did they contradict each other? Two clusters usually means two different buyers rather than one confused market, and separating them rescues more weeks than any other finding here.
4. **SEVERITY.** Judged on prior attempts, current spend, and remembered instances. Not on the self-reported number, and never on enthusiasm.
5. **BUDGET.** Who controls the money, and is that the person who feels the pain? Stage 1's payer block already recorded an answer. State it, state what the conversations showed, and name the difference: confirmed, wrong payer, or no payer. The second is a correction and often a better business; the third is a verdict.
6. **WHAT THE USER GOT WRONG.** Checked against the sentence they wrote at Step 1.

### The four verdicts and their recovery routes

| What was found | What to do |
|---|---|
| The problem is confirmed, in their words | Revise the Stage 1 packet, raise Desirability with the evidence named, raise its confidence, release any vantage cap the conversations resolved, update the problem source, and carry the quotes forward |
| The problem is real but a different one than expected | Rewrite the Stage 1 problem statement and UVP, then confirm the same buyer still holds. Same candidate. This is a good outcome and it is common. |
| The problem is real but nobody owns the budget | Test an alternate payer for the same problem first, starting from who Stage 1 assumed. Update the Stage 1 payer block and drop Viability confidence to Low until a payer is found. Only if none exists is the candidate rejected — then correct that row's Budget score in the Stage 0 packet with a ledger entry, and follow the row below. |
| Nobody has the problem | Reject the candidate: back to the Stage 0 ranked portfolio, mark the row rejected with date and evidence, confirm the next candidate with the user, archive the superseded Stage 1 packet to `usl-build/archive/`, and run a fresh Stage 1. If the portfolio is exhausted, re-run Stage 0 Steps 4 and 5 against the existing inventory instead. Better now than in November. |

Changing course this week is the system working, and it is far cheaper than changing it after something has been built. Say that plainly, because it will not feel that way to the user.

## SECTION 7: TRACK B, WHEN CONVERSATIONS ARE IMPOSSIBLE

Real constraints exist: an employer conflict, a regulated context, or a buyer population with no route to it yet. Not a failure, and it does not end the sprint.

Establish it explicitly rather than letting the week drift. Ask directly: is this a constraint, or is this the calendar being hard? The second one is not Track B, and switching too early costs the user the entire value of the week.

**The substitute, in order of usefulness:**

1. **Mine public sources for the language table. [Live research available]** Forums, subreddits, review sites, complaint threads and support communities are full of the buyer describing the problem in their own words to somebody else. This is genuinely usable and it is what Stage 4 Step 3 will suggest anyway. Three-star reviews outperform one-star reviews: the angry ones describe an incident, the ambivalent ones describe the workflow.
2. **Use past evidence the user already holds.** If they have lived this problem inside an organisation, there may be old tickets, post-mortems, threads, or handover notes. Anything a real person wrote at the time beats anything the user remembers now.
3. **Say yes to live research at every marked point in Stage 4.** It is designed for exactly this gap.

Write the same file with the same structure. Every finding carries `[public source]` with its URL and date, or `[past artifact]` with what it was. Section 1 states plainly that no live conversations were run. Section 9 carries the honest consequence: this is what people said to each other, not what they said to the user, and nobody was asked a follow-up question.

**What the substitute may and may not write into the Stage 1 packet.** This is where the record is easiest to inflate, so it is fixed:

| Field | What Track B may set it to |
|---|---|
| `Buyer conversations completed` | 0. Always. Never a count of sources read. |
| `Conversation sample` | `public-source substitute` |
| `Conversation status` | `substituted`, with the reason |
| Problem source | `public evidence` at most, and only where sources genuinely describe the problem. Never `direct experience` or `repeated observation`, which belong to the user, not to a forum. |
| Desirability confidence | Up by at most one level, never to High |
| Vantage cap | **Not released.** A cap set because the user has never been close to this problem is not retired by reading about it. |
| Payer block | Unchanged unless a source names the buying process explicitly. Public sources almost never establish budget authority. |

Stage 4 runs Track B and marks the offer reasoned rather than observed. That is honest, usable, and a better position than a confident guess.

## SECTION 8: WORKED EXAMPLES

Three conversations from the same week, on the same problem, to calibrate what the difference actually looks like. Offer the user an example **from a different field than their own**.

### Example 1: Strong signal

**Ops lead, 200-person manufacturer. Former colleague of the user.**

Problem under test: supplier onboarding takes too long.

The instance: "Last month. We had a supplier ready to go on the Tuesday and they did not ship until the 14th, because I was chasing insurance certificates through three inboxes."

What they had tried: built a shared spreadsheet with conditional formatting; hired a temp for six weeks last year; asked procurement to take it over and was refused.

Current spend: the temp cost about 4,000. Their own time, roughly a day a week.

Budget: "That would be Dianne, and she signed off the temp last year without much argument."

The quote that goes on a page: *"By the time I've chased three people for the paperwork, I've lost a week and I still can't start."*

**Why it is strong:** three prior attempts, money already spent, a named approver with a precedent, and a dated instance. Note that the user never asked whether the buyer would pay. They found out by asking what they already spend.

### Example 2: Weak signal

**Operations director, 60-person distributor. Warm introduction.**

The instance: "It is definitely a pain point for us. It comes up quite a lot."

Pressed for the last time it happened: "I could not tell you off the top of my head. It is more of a general friction thing."

What they had tried: nothing. "We have talked about it."

Current spend: none identified.

Budget: "It would probably go through finance, I suppose it depends."

They said: *"That sounds really useful, let me know when it is ready."*

**Why it is weak:** enthusiasm about the idea, vagueness about the situation, no attempts, no spend, no dated instance, and a hedged approver. This conversation felt better than the first one and is worth a fraction of it. If the user records this as strong because it was pleasant, the whole week is wasted.

### Example 3: The kill signal, and the recovery

**Plant manager, 400-person manufacturer. Cold.**

Asked for the last instance twice, and could not produce one: "Honestly, onboarding is not something I think about. Purchasing deals with it."

But then, unprompted: "The thing that actually costs me is when a supplier changes a spec and nobody tells the line until we have run half a batch."

**Why this is the most valuable conversation of the three:** the problem under test is dead for this buyer, and a real problem with a specific, expensive, remembered instance arrived in its place. This is the "real, but a different problem" verdict, and it is common. The correct move is to rewrite the problem statement, not to keep pushing onboarding because the user has already written it down.

Two things to notice for the debrief. First, the finding came after the buyer had been told the user's topic was not their topic, which is exactly when people relax and say the true thing. Second, it arrived because the user followed rule 5, followed the emotion, instead of moving to the next question on the list.
